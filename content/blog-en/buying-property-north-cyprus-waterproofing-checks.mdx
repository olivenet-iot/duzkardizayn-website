## What this covers, and what it does not

This is about the fabric of the building: the roof, the terraces, the balconies, the walls, the wet rooms and the pool. It is the part of the purchase nobody looks at properly, and it is the part that generates the largest unexpected bills in the first two or three years of ownership.

It is not about the legal side. Title, permits, contracts, payment schedules and everything connected with them are matters for a qualified independent lawyer, instructed by you and unconnected to the seller or the developer. Nothing here is a substitute for that.

We are a waterproofing and renovation contractor based in Alsancak, just outside Kyrenia, and we have been working on buildings across North Cyprus since 1999. A steady part of our work is being called in by people who have owned a property for eighteen months and hit their first serious winter. Most of what follows is the list of things we wish they had looked at first.

</section>

<section id="summer-viewing">

## The summer viewing problem

Most buyers see the property between April and October. That is when flights are convenient, when the island is at its best, and when every building in North Cyprus is dry.

Around 60 per cent of the annual rainfall arrives between December and February, in short intense bursts. A roof with no falls, a cracked parapet detail or a balcony with a failed membrane will behave perfectly all summer and give you nothing to see. The defect is still there. It simply has no water to demonstrate itself with.

So on a summer viewing you are not looking for water. You are looking for the marks water leaves behind, and for the repairs someone has made to hide them.

<InfoBox type="tip" title="Ask when it was last painted">
Fresh paint on one wall or one ceiling in an otherwise tired room is worth a direct question. Damp patches painted over typically come back within three to six months, so a patch that has been redecorated recently and looks perfect in July may well have been redecorated because of what appeared in February. It is not proof of anything, but it tells you where to look harder.
</InfoBox>

</section>

Buyers tend to assume newer means safer. In this market that is not reliably true. The two categories carry different risks, and they need different questions.

Two specific points. On newer developments the failure we see most often is a roof covered with one thin membrane layer and no properly formed falls, finished quickly at the end of the build. It gets through two or three winters and then lets go, which is exactly when the original developer has moved on.

On older stock, the issue is straightforward arithmetic. Buildings from the 1990s and earlier frequently had no waterproofing to current standards, and the bituminous materials used at the time have a working life of around 15 to 20 years. On anything built before about 2005, assume the original waterproofing is finished unless someone can show you otherwise.

<section id="questions">

## Questions to ask the developer or vendor

These are worth asking directly, in writing where possible. What you are testing is not just the answer but whether anyone knows it.

**On the roof and terraces:** what waterproofing system was used, how many layers and at what thickness? Were falls formed to the outlets, and at what percentage? Was the membrane dressed up the parapets, and how far? Was a water test carried out before the screed or tiles went down? Are there photographs from during construction?

**On the ground floor and any basement:** was the raft or foundation tanked, and from the outside or the inside? Was a drainage layer installed against the retaining walls? Does the property have a basement or semi-basement, and has water ever appeared in it?

**On the wet rooms:** were the bathrooms and the kitchen tanked underneath the tiling, or was the tiling laid straight onto screed? Tiles and grout are not a waterproofing layer, and a bathroom without tanking underneath will eventually show up in the room below.

**On history and warranty:** what waterproofing or roof work has been done since the building was completed, and by whom? Is any of it still under guarantee? Is the contractor who did it still trading?

**On the building as a whole, for an apartment:** who is responsible for the roof, and is there a maintenance fund? Under the condominium arrangements here, waterproofing of a balcony is generally the responsibility of the owner of that flat, and an owner whose balcony leaks into the property below can be liable for the damage. Ask whether any such dispute is running in the block.

<InfoBox type="warning" title="A vague answer is an answer">
"It is fully waterproofed" and "there has never been a problem" are not answers, they are reassurances. Anyone who built or maintained the property properly can tell you what system was used and show you something. The absence of any record is itself information, and it should feed into your offer.
</InfoBox>

</section>

<section id="before-you-sign">

## Before you sign

The whole point of looking early is that everything on this list is cheaper to know about than to discover. A roof, a balcony, a basement or a pool can all be put right, and the work is not exotic. What ruins the first year of ownership is the surprise, not the repair.

Three things worth doing before you commit:

- Get onto the roof. If nothing else on this list happens, do that one.
- Ask the waterproofing questions in writing, and keep the answers.
- Have someone who works on buildings for a living walk round it with you, particularly on anything over about ten years old.
