<section id="the-call">

## The knock on the door

On a wet morning in January the neighbour downstairs knocks. There is water dripping from their ceiling, and they are certain it is coming from your balcony. Or you notice it from your own side first: damp patches under the tiles that never quite dry out, even through a dry week.

It is one of the most common winter problems on this island, and among British and other foreign owners it turns into something worse than a repair job. One of you is very likely to be off the island. There may be no on-site management, no factor, nobody with authority to make a decision. Messages go back and forth between the UK and Kyrenia, nobody wants to accept it is theirs, and meanwhile water keeps going into the slab every time it rains.

The useful part is that balcony leaks are well understood. Around 80 per cent of balcony repairs come down to a failure of the waterproofing system, and the failures repeat: five causes, a small number of construction details that decide everything, and one repair that actually works against several that only look like repairs.

## Is it the balcony, or is it plumbing?

Answer this before anybody spends money, because a ceiling stain is evidence of water, not evidence of a balcony.

Two checks anyone can do without a specialist. First, turn off every tap and appliance in the upstairs flat, read the water meter, then read it again a couple of hours later. Movement means a plumbing leak somewhere regardless of what the ceiling looks like. Second, go out onto the balcony after rain and look for standing water. If water is still lying there a couple of days later, the falls are wrong and you have found the most likely cause without any equipment at all.

One complication worth knowing. Water travels. It goes into the slab at one point, runs along the top of a beam and comes out somewhere else, which is why the wet patch on the ceiling below is often not underneath the actual defect. Chasing the stain upwards rarely lands on the right spot.

<section id="why-below-first">

## Why it damages the flat below first

The reason the neighbour notices before you do is that a balcony leak works downwards through the structure and the visible damage collects at the bottom.

Water leaving the balcony surface soaks into the concrete slab. Concrete is porous, so it takes the water up and passes it slowly down to the beams and columns beneath. At that stage the reinforcement inside starts to corrode and there is nothing at all to see. Rust occupies more volume than the steel it came from, so as it forms it cracks the concrete from the inside and begins to break it up. Only at the end of that sequence do the yellow-brown stains, damp and eventually falling plaster appear on the ceiling below.

That whole process from the first leak to the first ceiling stain can take **1 to 3 years**. Which means two things. The neighbour complaining now is reporting something that started long before, and the concrete has had that whole period of moisture working on it. It also means that if you are the owner downstairs, a stain is not a decorating problem to be painted over next spring. It is the visible end of a structural process.

<InfoBox type="warning" title="A ceiling stain is a late symptom">
By the time water shows on the ceiling below, moisture has been in the slab for a long period and the reinforcement has had the same exposure. Redecorating the ceiling before the balcony above is dealt with is money spent twice, because it will come back with the next winter's rain. <section id="five-causes">

## The five things that cause it

Nearly every balcony leak we open up comes down to these five, and usually to two or three of them at once.

### 1. Inadequate fall

The balcony floor needs a minimum fall of 1.5 to 2 per cent towards the outlet, which is 1:60 to 1:80. Once the gradient drops below 1:100, water stands on the balcony for 48 to 72 hours after rain and the leak risk multiplies, because standing water finds its way down through micro-cracks and grout lines under sustained pressure. This is the most common cause of all. On older buildings the fall was often never calculated, and on others it has flattened over time.

No membrane solves a falls problem. The right membrane over the wrong fall still leaves water standing on the balcony.

### 2. The door threshold detail

The junction between the balcony door threshold and the floor is the most critical water barrier on the whole balcony, and it is a small, fiddly detail that takes patience to get right. The mastic or joint filler used there hardens and cracks under UV, and once it has, rain runs under the threshold and straight into the room. Given the UV levels here, this point needs attention on average every 5 to 7 years.

### 3. The outlet

Where the balcony outlet meets the pipe, if the connection was left without a membrane collar or sleeve dressed into it, that is an open door for water. Add years of dirt and plant debris blocking the outlet itself, and the water that cannot drain has only one route left, which is down through the floor. This is the classic finding on a balcony nobody has maintained.

### 4. A worn membrane

Even a correctly installed balcony waterproofing system has a finite life. A balcony membrane is directly exposed to UV, and the expected life here is 10 to 15 years, against 15 to 20 years on a roof and 20 to 25 years below ground. Cracked by UV and fatigued by daily thermal cycling, it stops holding water. The damage is under the tiles and invisible from above, which is why balconies tend to be diagnosed by their symptoms downstairs rather than by inspection.

### 5. Neglected movement joints

The corner joints between the floor and the wall are deliberate gaps left for thermal movement. When the filler in them loses its flexibility, or the joint opens completely, you have a direct route in. These joints need a flexible mastic rather than a rigid filler, and renewing every 5 to 7 years.

### Why this happens faster here

Solar radiation in North Cyprus is roughly 2 to 3 times the Central European level. A balcony floor in direct summer sun reaches a surface temperature of 60 to 70°C, putting the concrete, the tiles and the membrane underneath through tens of degrees of thermal cycling every single day. Every expansion and contraction cycle works slowly at the joints, the corner junctions and the membrane surface.

Then the winter arrives in short intense bursts rather than steady drizzle. Between November and March, 40 to 60 mm of rain can fall in a matter of hours, which on a balcony with insufficient fall becomes standing water very quickly.

<InfoBox type="info" title="A Kyrenia balcony is not a German balcony">
Take two balconies built to the same standard with the same materials, one in Germany and one in Kyrenia. The German one may go 20 years without trouble while the Cypriot one needs attention in 10 to 12 years. The difference is the UV dose, the number of thermal cycles and the sea salt. On the coastal neighbourhoods around Kyrenia, salt air works into the pores of the balcony concrete, breaks down its crystal structure, widens the pores and raises its permeability, while accelerating corrosion of the reinforcement.
</InfoBox>

## Why coatings over the existing tiles fail

The offer is tempting, particularly if you are handling it remotely: nobody lifts anything, the balcony stays usable, and a liquid coating goes over the existing tiles. It is quick and it is cheap and it fails, for three reasons.

It bonds to the wrong thing. The coating adheres to the glazed tile surface rather than to the concrete, so it is only ever as well attached as the tiles are, and the tiles are already sitting on a failed system.

It leaves the problem in place. Whatever is wrong beneath the tiles, saturated screed, a split membrane, an unsleeved outlet, is sealed in rather than removed. Trapped moisture then works against the new coating from underneath.

It does not change the falls. If the water was standing before, it will stand on the new surface too, and standing water is what caused the failure in the first place.

There is one sensible use for a temporary measure: buying a few months when the wet season has already started and the work cannot be done properly until spring. As a solution it is not one.

<section id="whose-job">

## Whose responsibility is it in a block?

This is where the dispute usually sits, and it is worth being careful about, because the answer is not the same in every building.

As a general principle, a balcony slab is part of the structure of the building rather than a private fitting like a kitchen cupboard. Leaks that pass from one flat into another are therefore usually treated as a shared or structural matter rather than as a purely private redecoration issue. But how the cost is actually apportioned depends on the individual building's own arrangements: what the title deeds say, what any management or maintenance agreement provides for, and what the block has agreed among the owners. Some buildings have a maintenance fund that covers exactly this. Many older blocks around Kyrenia have nothing of the sort.

Two pieces of practical advice. First, read the paperwork before the argument starts: the deed, any management agreement, and any block rules. Second, if a real sum of money is at stake, or the other party is refusing to engage, take local legal advice rather than relying on what anyone asserts at a residents' meeting. We are contractors, not lawyers, and we will not tell you what the law says.

What we can do is establish, in writing and with photographs, where the water is coming from and what it will take to stop it. In a disputed case that document is usually the thing that moves the conversation forward, because most disagreements between neighbours here are not really about liability. They are about the fact that nobody has established what is actually wrong.

<InfoBox type="tip" title="Get the diagnosis in writing early">
Where two owners disagree, an independent survey setting out the cause, with photographs, is worth more than months of messages. Both parties can then argue about a documented fact rather than about opinions, and in our experience the repair usually gets agreed once the cause is no longer in question.
</InfoBox>

<section id="timing">

## Timing, and what to do if you are not on the island

The best periods for balcony waterproofing here are **March to May** and **September to November**, when temperatures sit at 15 to 28°C, the humidity is balanced and the risk of rain is low.

The two constraints are moisture and heat. Waterproofing should not be applied in rain or below 5°C, and the surface has to be genuinely dry, which after rain means waiting at least 48 hours. At the other end, in July and August the balcony surface can pass 60°C, at which point the membrane and screed materials dry far too fast and crack, so summer work is done early in the morning or on shaded elevations. The mild Cypriot winter means the work is technically possible at most times of year, but December to February is the season you want to be finished before, not the season to start in.

The practical implication is straightforward. If your neighbour complained last winter, the work wants booking for autumn, not for next January when it will be done in poor conditions on a slab that is never quite dry.

### If you are an absent owner

- Get the diagnosis done now rather than waiting until you are next out. A survey needs access to the balcony, not to you.
- Ask for photographs of the cause, not just of the symptom. Standing water after rain, the threshold joint, the outlet.
- Ask whoever holds your keys to walk through and photograph ceilings after the first serious rain of the winter, in your flat and, if you can arrange it, in the one below.
- Get the scope in writing before agreeing anything, particularly if the cost is going to be split with another owner.
- Do not let a redecoration go ahead downstairs before the balcony above has been repaired. It will need doing twice.
