<section id="how-much-is-normal">

## Every pool loses water. How much is too much?

Every open pool in Cyprus loses water in summer. Sun, heat, wind and low humidity take it off the surface all day, and if the pool is uncovered and exposed, it goes faster still. So the fact that you are topping up does not on its own mean anything is wrong.

What you cannot do is judge it by eye. Evaporation here varies enormously from week to week depending on wind, humidity and whether the pool is covered, so "it seems like more than last month" is not evidence. Neither is a figure from a website written for a different climate.

The useful thing is that you do not need a number at all. You need a comparison, in the same weather, on the same day, and that is exactly what the bucket test gives you.

Worth doing it properly, because the cost of ignoring a real leak is not the water. It is what the water does on its way out. Escaping water goes into the ground around and beneath the pool. On the sloping plots that most villas around Kyrenia sit on, it tracks downhill towards the house, and it frequently turns up as damp in a lower ground floor or basement long before anyone connects the two. It also washes out the ground supporting the pool surround and, eventually, the pool.

<section id="where-it-stops">

## What the level tells you

If the bucket test confirms a leak, the next question is where, and the pool will often tell you if you stop topping it up and let it find its own level.

Water can only drop to the height of the hole. Where it stops is therefore roughly where the leak is.

- **Stops level with the skimmer mouth:** suspect the skimmer body or its junction with the pool wall. This is one of the most common leak points of all.
- **Stops at an underwater light:** suspect the light niche and its conduit, where the seal has failed or was never made properly.
- **Stops at a specific line partway down the wall:** look at that line. A crack, a failed movement joint or a pipe inlet at that height is the usual answer.
- **Keeps dropping below all the fittings:** the leak is in the shell itself, the floor, or the pipework below water level.
- **Loses nothing when the pump is off:** the pipework under pressure is the place to look, not the pool.

Other supporting signs are worth noting. Permanently soft, wet or unusually green ground on one side of the pool. Settlement or cracking in the paving around the surround. Air being pulled into the pump, or a drop in circulation pressure. Damp appearing in a basement or a lower ground floor room on the same side of the plot.

</section>

<section id="why-pools-fail-here">

## Why pools fail here

A pool in North Cyprus is under pressure from both directions at once, which is the thing that makes this climate harder on pools than most.

**From the inside**, the water itself is aggressive. Pool water runs at pH 7.2 to 7.8 with a chlorine concentration of 1 to 3 ppm, and that is a chemical environment that ordinary waterproofing is not designed for. Standard cement-based waterproofing products, the sort used in a bathroom, break down in chlorinated water within three to five years. If a bathroom product was used on a pool, it will fail, regardless of who applied it.

**From the outside**, winter rain between November and February raises the ground water table, and that water presses inwards against the pool shell. A pool waterproofed only on the inside face has nothing resisting that pressure from behind, and negative pressure of this kind is a common reason pools that were fine all summer start leaking after a wet winter.

**On top of both**, the thermal cycle. Summer air temperatures in the Kyrenia area reach 38 to 42°C during the day and fall to 22 to 25°C at night. That daily swing of 15 to 20 degrees runs the pool concrete through hundreds of expansion and contraction cycles a year, and the movement concentrates at movement joints and pipe penetrations, which is precisely where micro-cracks appear.

Add the UV load, which peaks at an index of 11 in July and is still at 9 in April and May, and the chloride from sea air on coastal plots, which drives corrosion of reinforcement, and you have a structure being attacked from four directions.

<InfoBox type="warning" title="Inside waterproofing alone is not enough here">
Because the pressure comes from both sides, a pool needs waterproofing on both. Internally, a crystalline system that reacts with moisture to grow crystals inside the pores of the concrete, becoming part of the structure rather than a coating that can peel. Externally, on the faces in contact with the ground, primer and a double layer of bituminous membrane. One without the other leaves a route open.
</InfoBox>

The general rule holds firmly with pools: dealing with a leak after it starts costs several times what building the waterproofing correctly would have cost, because you pay to find it, to strip and replace the finish, and often to repair whatever the escaping water damaged elsewhere on the plot.

On timing, new pool waterproofing runs to about 5 to 7 days, and a renovation of an existing pool 7 to 10 days, with the water test and curing bringing the total to as much as two weeks. Work needs air temperatures between 8 and 30°C and no rain, which in practice makes spring and autumn the comfortable seasons, with high summer workable if the crystalline application is kept out of the middle of the day.

<InfoBox type="warning" title="Do not drain the pool for the winter">
Between November and February the ground water level rises. A full pool balances that pressure from the inside. An empty shell in that period is resisting ground water with nothing pushing back, and can crack or lift. If the pool has to be emptied for work, the timing and the ground conditions are planned around, rather than the pool simply being left standing empty through the wet season.
</InfoBox>

If your pool is losing water, do the bucket test first. It costs nothing and it tells you whether you have a problem at all. If it confirms a leak, the next step is finding it, and that is worth doing before it starts undermining the surround or showing up as damp in the house.
